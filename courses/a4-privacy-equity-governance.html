Part 1 · covers FERPA/COPPA guidance + Singapore MOE/PDPA

Three regimes, four rails 8 min live

You do not need to become a privacy lawyer - you need to know that three very different legal regimes, written decades apart on two continents, all land on the same four operational rules. Set those four rails once and you are substantially compliant everywhere your students are. Skip them and every helpful teacher with a browser tab is a disclosure incident waiting to happen.

LiveFERPA: the school-official rail4 min▶

FERPA's core for leaders: student PII from education records entered into a consumer AI tool is an unauthorized disclosure - unless the vendor qualifies as a "school official" under a written agreement, the school keeps control of the data, and the vendor does not reuse it or train on it. A teacher's personal ChatGPT account meets none of those tests.

  • The practical rail: never student PII in consumer chatbots - grades, IEPs, behavior notes, health information, even a name attached to a struggle.
  • The sanctioned path: district-vetted tools operating under data-processing agreements (DPAs) that lock in no-reuse, no-training terms. The teacher's workflow barely changes; the legal exposure changes completely.
  • The state layer on top: roughly 12 US states now explicitly say do not input student PII into AI systems, and about 20 anchor their AI guidance on FERPA, COPPA, CIPA, and IDEA. The direction of travel is one way.

The leader's question is not "do my teachers know FERPA?" It is "have I given them a vetted tool, so the compliant path is also the easy path?"

LiveCOPPA and the under-13 reality3 min▶

COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under 13, and schools may consent on parents' behalf only for educational use. Meanwhile the consumer chatbot terms of service are 13+ across the board (OpenAI: 13+, and 13-18 with parental consent).

  • What that means bluntly: a Grade 4 class "trying out ChatGPT" on student accounts is outside both the vendor's own terms and the consent framework - before any pedagogy is discussed.
  • The workable pattern: elementary AI use is teacher-mediated or runs in walled-garden tools built for schools - the teacher drives, the students never hold consumer accounts.
Real world

Singapore built the walled garden at national scale. MOE routes classroom AI through the Student Learning Space (SLS) with guardrails built in - the AI Learning Assistant "LEA" is available from Primary 4, teacher-supervised for P4-6, and student data stays under PDPA protection with minors' consent handled via parents and guardians. Nobody asks each teacher to be a privacy lawyer; the platform IS the policy. That is the design target for any district, at any scale.

Self-studyThe 9pm test: what counts as student PII3 min read▶

The rail only works if a tired teacher can apply it at 9pm without a flowchart. The test: could anyone connect this to a real student? If yes, it stays out of anything not on the vetted list.

  • Obviously in scope: names, student IDs, grades, IEP and 504 details, health or counseling notes, disciplinary history, contact details, photos.
  • The sneaky middle: "my Grade 9 student with dyslexia who just moved from Manila" - no name, still identifiable in a class of 28. Combinations of details are PII even when each detail alone is not.
  • Out of scope, and encouraged: fully de-identified patterns - "a ninth grader struggling with topic sentences" - which is all the AI needed to draft the support plan anyway.

That last point is the training message that actually lands: the compliant prompt is usually the BETTER prompt, because the model never needed the name - only the situation. Teach the de-identified habit once and the rail stops feeling like a restriction.

Part 2 · covers the TeachAI toolkit + the equity gap data

The governance toolkit you do not have to write 7 min live

The good news for a busy leadership team: the starting documents already exist, free, and co-authored by the organizations your board will recognize. Your job is adoption and adaptation, not authorship - and the operational core is smaller than it looks: an approved-tool list and the agreements behind it.

LiveTeachAI: the off-the-shelf starting kit4 min▶

The TeachAI toolkit - a coalition effort from Code.org, ETS, ISTE, Khan Academy, and the World Economic Forum - hands you three artifacts most institutions try to write from a blank page:

  • 7 guiding principles for AI in education - the values layer your policy points back to.
  • Sample policy language - editable text for acceptable use, disclosure, and staff guidance, ready for your counsel to mark up rather than draft.
  • A transparency guide - how to tell students, parents, and staff what AI is in use and why.

Then the operational core, where governance becomes real: the approved-tool list plus DPAs. A living list of vetted tools, each backed by a written agreement covering PII handling, no-reuse, no-training. Everything else in AI governance is commentary on those two artifacts - the list tells teachers what the easy path is, and the agreements make the easy path legal.

The questions for your next leadership meeting: Who owns our approved list? What is the request-and-review process when a teacher finds a new tool? How fast does a decision come back - because if the answer is "months", the shadow tools win.

LiveThe equity section: ungoverned AI is not neutral3 min▶

Here is the number that turns governance from an IT chore into a leadership obligation: only 18% of US principals say their school provides AI guidance - and the split is 13% in high-poverty schools versus 25% in affluent ones.

★ The equity argument, in one breath Ungoverned AI is an equity failure. The schools least likely to have rails serve the students least able to push back - on a false AI accusation, on a privacy disclosure, on a consumer tool quietly training on their data. Affluent schools are twice as likely to have guidance; their students get the protections AND the productive use. Waiting to govern does not pause the risk - it distributes it to the students with the least recourse. If your institution serves a high-need population, this session is not optional homework. It is the job.

Pair that with a3's detection evidence and the pattern is complete: the same students detectors falsely flag - non-native writers, under-resourced schools - are the ones no policy protects. Governance is how a leader closes both gaps with one document.

Self-studyThe governance one-pager: six lines, one page4 min read▶

Your entire AI governance posture should fit on one page a new teacher can absorb in five minutes. The six-line checklist:

  • 1 · Policy per course. Every syllabus states its AI tier (a3's mandate). Owner: academic leadership.
  • 2 · Vetted-tool list. One living page of approved tools; anything off-list needs a request, not forgiveness. Owner: named, with a review deadline per request.
  • 3 · DPA template. Standard agreement terms - school-official status, no reuse, no training - so vetting is a checklist, not a negotiation. Owner: counsel or procurement.
  • 4 · The PII rule. One sentence, no exceptions: no student personal information in any tool that is not on the list.
  • 5 · Incident protocol. What happens when PII lands in the wrong tool anyway: report without blame, contain, notify per regulation, fix the workflow that made it easy.
  • 6 · Review cadence. The list, the policy, and the incidents get reviewed on a named schedule - this space moves too fast for annual.

Draft it this week; a5 slots it into the full institutional roadmap alongside training and build-vs-buy decisions.

Demo · the privacy trace

Watch the IEP paste happen - then watch the rails catch it ★ 12 min · everyone runs it

The simulator below runs the incident this whole session exists to prevent: a caring, competent teacher pastes a student's IEP details into a consumer chatbot to draft a support plan faster. Every lever is off - which is exactly the state of a school with no guidance, the 82% majority in the principal survey. Nobody in this trace is careless or malicious. That is what makes it the scariest scenario in the course.

Run it as-is, all levers off. Follow the teacher's reasoning - every step feels helpful and normal right up until the protected record is sitting in a consumer tool that may train on it.

Note what is missing: not good intentions, and not effort. There was no vetted tool to reach for, no PII rule to remember, no agreement making any path safe. The failure was built before the teacher sat down.

Toggle Privacy rails ON and run again. Same teacher, same 9pm, same need - the vetted tool with a DPA does the same job, and the disclosure never happens.

Say the leader lesson out loud: you cannot train your way out of a missing rail. The fix is infrastructure - a list, an agreement, one rule - not a memo asking teachers to be more careful.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Under FERPA, when does entering student PII into an AI tool avoid being an unauthorized disclosure?

Intent and deletion are irrelevant to the disclosure. The school-official exception is a contractual state, not a vibe - which is why the operational rail is district-vetted tools with DPAs, never consumer chatbots.

2 · A Grade 4 teacher wants students to use a consumer chatbot directly. What is the under-13 reality?

Schools may consent only for educational use, and the vendors' own terms exclude under-13 accounts anyway. The workable pattern is the teacher driving, or a school-built walled garden - Singapore's SLS with LEA from Primary 4 is the national-scale version.

3 · What does the principal-survey data say about who has AI guidance?

The gap is the point: the schools least likely to have rails serve the students least able to push back on a false accusation or a privacy disclosure. Ungoverned AI is an equity failure, and governance is how a leader closes the gap.
