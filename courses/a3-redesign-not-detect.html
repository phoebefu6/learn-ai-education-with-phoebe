Detection is broken - redesign instead

Somewhere in your institution right now, someone is proposing to buy an AI detector. This session is the 45 minutes that saves you from signing that purchase order. The verified record is brutal: OpenAI retired its own detector at a 26% catch rate, Stanford researchers found detectors falsely flag 61%+ of honest essays by non-native English speakers, and Vanderbilt did the arithmetic - at their scale, a "1% false-positive rate" means roughly 750 wrongly accused students every year - then switched the detector off. Tonight you see the false accusation manufactured live in the simulator, and you leave with the move that actually works: mandate a policy per course, not a detector per school.

LiveRead the record like a risk officer4 min▶

Strip the edtech language away and a detector is a screening test with a known error profile, run at scale, whose positive results trigger disciplinary proceedings. Three questions any risk officer would ask:

  • What is the base-rate exposure? Multiply the false-positive rate by your annual submission volume. At Vanderbilt's scale, even the marketing number produced ~750 accusations of innocent students a year. Run your own number before any meeting where the purchase is discussed.
  • Who absorbs the errors? Not randomly distributed students - the Stanford data says non-native English speakers absorb them at 61%+, and the accused cannot prove a negative. There is no exculpatory evidence for "I just wrote it."
  • What does the true positive buy you? In every documented case pattern, the genuinely outsourced work was already catchable by process evidence - drafts, in-class baselines, a five-minute conversation. The detector's unique contribution is the false accusations.

That is the whole case. The tool fails the students you are most obligated to protect, and succeeds only where you did not need it.

Self-studyThe claim vs the fine print3 min read▶

When a vendor deck reaches your desk, three claims will be on it. The honest annotations:

  • "99%+ accuracy." Accuracy is the wrong metric for rare events - a detector that flags nothing at all scores high accuracy. Ask for the false-positive rate at YOUR volume, in accusations per year.
  • "Under 1% false positives." Turnitin's own admission: that figure is document-level for high-scoring documents; sentence-level false positives ran ~4%, with lower reliability below its thresholds.
  • "Constantly improving." The evasion side improves faster. Liang's team cut flags from 61% to 12% with a single rewrite prompt. You would be buying a permanent arms race in which the honest students are the casualties.

Keep this page open in that meeting. Every number on it is published and citable.

Self-studyScripts for the three pushbacks you will hear3 min read▶

When you table this evidence, three objections arrive on schedule. Ready answers, in a leader's voice:

  • "Doing nothing feels irresponsible." Agreed - which is why the alternative is not nothing. It is a stated policy in every syllabus plus evidence-based assessment, which catches outsourced work better than the detector did, without the ~750 false accusations.
  • "Other schools use detectors." The schools that published their reasoning - Vanderbilt, Michigan State, Northwestern - switched them off. Ask the schools still running one for their false-accusation count; they will not have measured it.
  • "Our teachers want backup on suspicion." Then give them backup that works: the in-class baseline, the draft trail, the five-minute conversation script. A detector score is not backup - it is a liability with a dashboard.

Notice the pattern in all three replies: never argue AI ethics in the abstract. Argue accusations per year, evidence per case, and who absorbs the errors. Those are numbers a board can act on.

Part 2 · covers Vanderbilt, Harvard, Stanford syllabus policy patterns

What leading institutions mandate instead 7 min live

The same institutions that switched detectors off did not go soft on integrity - they moved the control point. Instead of one tool policing every submission, every course states its own rules up front. The pattern is remarkably consistent across Vanderbilt, Harvard, and Stanford, and it is cheap to mandate.

LiveThe 3-tier syllabus policy pattern4 min▶

Vanderbilt's guidance gives faculty three exact tiers to choose from, per course, per assignment:

  • Allowed - AI use is permitted, students disclose how they used it.
  • Allowed only in certain scenarios - the syllabus names which tasks and which uses; everything else is off the table.
  • Never allowed - and the assessment is designed so that the rule is enforceable without a detector.

Harvard runs the same idea with different labels - "maximally restrictive" / "mixed" / "fully-encouraging" - and Harvard FAS requires every syllabus to state one. Stanford adds two sharpening rules: the statement must be self-contained (a student reading only the syllabus knows exactly what is allowed), and students cite AI use the way they would cite any source.

Real world

Why per-course beats school-wide. A single blanket rule is always wrong somewhere: the coding course wants AI pair work the literature seminar forbids. The tier menu keeps the DECISION with the person who owns the learning outcome - the instructor - while the institution mandates only that a decision gets made and published. That is governance doing its actual job.

LiveThe leader's move: mandate a policy per course, not a detector per school3 min▶

One sentence of institutional policy replaces the entire detector budget: "Every course states its AI tier in the syllabus, and suspicion is handled through evidence and conversation, never through detector output alone." What that one sentence buys you:

  • Due process. When 14 polished drafts show up, the question is "which tier did each student operate in?" - answerable with evidence - not "did AI touch this text?" - which no tool can answer.
  • Consistency for students. Honesty stops being a confession. A student who disclosed AI use within tier has followed the rules, by definition.
  • Zero procurement. The policy costs a faculty meeting and a template. The detector costs money, an arms race, and roughly one false accusation per hundred papers.

The questions to bring back to your leadership team: Which of our courses currently state any AI rule at all? Who owns the template? What is our deadline for 100% syllabus coverage?

Self-studyThe false-accusation protocol4 min read▶

Even with detectors gone, teachers will sometimes suspect. Your institution needs a written answer to "what happens next?" - because the improvised version is where the damage happens. The protocol that works:

  • Suspicion starts a conversation, not a proceeding. "Your final is much stronger than your in-class work - walk me through how you got there." Curious, not accusatory.
  • Evidence means process evidence. Drafts, outlines, the in-class baseline, a short oral walkthrough. If the course collected none of these, that is a course-design gap, not a student-guilt signal.
  • Detector output is never proof. Write this down explicitly: a detector score alone cannot open or sustain an integrity case. The published error rates make it inadmissible on its own terms.
  • The burden stays on the institution. A student cannot prove they did not use AI. If your process requires them to, your process is broken - that asymmetry is precisely why Vanderbilt pulled the plug.

Put this protocol in the same document as the tier mandate. Together they are your whole integrity architecture.

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Vanderbilt processed about 75,000 papers a year. At Turnitin's claimed 1% false-positive rate, what did their arithmetic show?

1% x 75,000 = ~750 innocent students facing accusations they cannot disprove, every single year. Vanderbilt switched the detector off in August 2023; Michigan State and Northwestern reached the same conclusion.

2 · The Stanford study in Patterns found detector errors are not randomly distributed. Who absorbs them?

Detectors scored near-perfectly on native-speaker essays and flagged 61%+ of honest non-native writing - while one rewrite prompt dropped real AI text from 61% to 12% flags. The tool punishes the honest and misses the prepared.

3 · What is the leader's move this session recommends in place of buying a detector?

The Vanderbilt / Harvard / Stanford pattern: every course states its tier (allowed / allowed only in certain scenarios / never allowed), the statement is self-contained, students cite AI use - and detector output is never proof. Governance moves the decision, not the tool.
