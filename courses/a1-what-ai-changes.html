Part 1 · covers Gallup/Walton 2025 + EDUCAUSE 2025 + TeachAI/RAND

The adoption reality 15 min live

Start with what is already true. Gallup's 2025 survey of 2,232 US public-school teachers: 60% used AI this school year, 32% use it weekly, and the weekly users save about 5.9 hours a week - roughly six working weeks a school year. The top uses are all preparation work: preparing to teach (37%), worksheets (33%), modifying materials (28%). Students moved even faster: 86% report using AI in their studies (Digital Education Council 2025 - secondary-sourced, so quote it as reported, not verified). Now the other side. EDUCAUSE 2025 (higher ed, n≈800): 57% call AI a strategic priority, but only 22% have an institution-wide AI strategy and only 39% have acceptable-use policies. Adoption arrived years before governance.

How to use this picture Put your own institution's three governance numbers next to the bars before the next leadership meeting. The point is not to shame anyone - 78% of institutions are in the same place - it is that every blue bar keeps growing on its own, and only decisions move the red ones.
LiveWhat your teachers are already doing with the time5 min▶

The Gallup numbers are worth reading twice, because they are the most solid thing in this whole space:

  • 60% adoption, 32% weekly - AI use in your school is no longer an early-adopter story. It is the median teacher.
  • 5.9 hours a week saved by weekly users - about six working weeks a school year, per teacher. That is the size of the prize your teachers have already found on their own.
  • Where the time comes from: preparing to teach (37%), worksheets (33%), modifying materials (28%). All preparation work - the low-risk, teacher-reviewed layer. Almost none of it is student-facing yet.
Real world

The leadership question is not "should we allow AI." That question was answered by your staff months ago, quietly. The question is whether the six weeks per teacher get reinvested deliberately - feedback, relationships, better assessment - or evaporate into whatever fills a vacuum. Institutions with a strategy get to choose. The other 78% do not.

LiveThe governance gap - and who it lands on5 min▶

Three numbers describe the institutional side, and none of them are flattering:

  • 57% vs 22% - EDUCAUSE 2025: 57% of institutions call AI a strategic priority, but only 22% have an institution-wide strategy. The intent-to-execution gap is 35 points wide.
  • 39% acceptable-use policies - up from 23% the year before, so the direction is right. But it still means most institutions leave every teacher and student to guess what is allowed.
  • 18% of principals' schools provide AI guidance (TeachAI/RAND) - and it splits 13% at high-poverty schools vs 25% at affluent ones. Governance is arriving fastest where students already have the most support. Doing nothing is not neutral; it widens the gap.

Where guidance is missing, the vacuum gets filled anyway - by TikTok advice, by vendor marketing, by each teacher's private policy. a4 turns the equity number into a governance decision; a5 gives you the roadmap.

Self-studyReading adoption surveys like a leader4 min read▶

You will be handed a new AI-adoption statistic roughly monthly from here on. Four checks keep you from repeating a bad one in a board paper:

  • Who was asked, and how many? Gallup 2025 is 2,232 US K-12 public-school teachers; EDUCAUSE 2025 is ~800 higher-ed respondents. Different populations - do not average them, and say which one you are quoting.
  • Measured behavior or self-report? All of tonight's adoption numbers are self-reported. Fine for direction and scale, weaker for precision - the 60% is sturdier as "a solid majority" than as a decimal.
  • Primary or secondary source? The student 86% reached us through citation chains, not the raw study. One label - "as reported" - keeps you honest at zero cost.
  • How old is it? This space moves in months. The acceptable-use number jumped from 23% to 39% in a single year; anything older than a year is history, not data.

These four checks are the leader-track version of Discernment - the same habit your teachers learn for AI output, applied to the numbers people put in front of you.

Self-studyThe student number, handled honestly3 min read▶

The 86% of students report using AI figure (54% weekly) comes from the Digital Education Council's 2025 survey and reaches us secondary-sourced - we verified the citation trail, not the raw study. Two disciplines for using it:

  • Label it "as reported" in any slide or board paper. Leaders who quote soft numbers as hard ones spend their credibility fast - and this track will keep separating measured from reported from hyped.
  • Its direction is safe even if its precision is not. Every source agrees student adoption exceeds teacher adoption, which exceeds institutional readiness. Your students are the most AI-active group in the building and the least guided.

That ordering - students ahead of teachers ahead of institutions - is the single most useful sentence from this session for your next leadership meeting.

Part 2 · previews Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 - full treatment in a2

The answer-machine problem + your institution 24 min live

Here is the one classroom story every leader needs before touching any AI decision. The best-designed study in this space (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 - a randomized trial with about 1,000 students) gave one group a vanilla chatbot. Their practice scores jumped +48% and everyone felt great - then they scored about 17% worse than the no-AI group on the unassisted exam. The AI had become an answer machine: it did the work, so the learning never happened. A second group got the same model wrapped in guardrails - hints, never direct answers. Practice improved +127% and the exam harm disappeared. Same tool, same students, opposite outcomes. Hold that story; a2 gives you the full evidence base in plain English.

Tell the adoption story first: 60% of teachers, six weeks a year saved. Lead with the upside - it is real and it is measured.

Then the answer-machine story: +48% practice, -17% exam. One sentence, one citation (PNAS 2025).

Then the reversal: same model, guardrails on, +127% and no harm. The variable is design, not the tool.

End on the gap: 22% have a strategy. Ask which side of that number your institution wants to be on. That is the whole board brief.

LiveWhat this means for your institution6 min▶

Three institutional consequences follow directly from the answer-machine story:

  • The unit of decision is design, not tools. The PNAS trial used the same model in both arms. Approving or banning "AI" misses the variable that actually moved the outcome - the design wrapped around it.
  • "Feels like learning" cannot be your metric. The harmed students were the happy ones: +48% in practice while learning quietly stopped. Any rollout measured on satisfaction and engagement alone is flying on the deceptive instrument.
  • Your policy vacuum is currently choosing "vanilla chatbot" for you. With no guidance - the 78% and the 61% - students default to the answer machine, because it is the path of least resistance. Not deciding is a decision, and it is the measured-harm one.
Real world

At Riverview, 14 of Ms. Tan's 28 final drafts read far above the class's in-term writing. No policy, no process evidence, no good options: accuse, ignore, or re-test - all losing moves. Your teachers meet that exact moment in their track's simulator. Every leader decision in a3-a6 exists so that moment stops being a crisis and becomes a procedure.

LiveFour moves NOT to make this quarter4 min▶

Most early AI damage in institutions comes from confident wrong moves, not from waiting. Four to take off the table tonight:

  • Do not buy an AI detector to "solve integrity". The evidence says detection is measurably broken, and the failure mode is false accusations of honest students. All of a3 is this story - hold the purchase order until then.
  • Do not ban AI and declare the problem handled. With 60% of teachers and 86% of students (as reported) already using it, a ban does not stop use - it stops disclosure. You lose the visibility and keep the risk.
  • Do not pilot a raw chatbot "to see what happens". The unguarded arm is the one with measured harm. If you pilot anything, pilot a designed use with a named guardrail - a2 gives you the exact language.
  • Do not put unlabeled numbers in front of your board. Measured, reported, and hyped are different substances. Leaders who label them get believed on everything else.
Self-studyThe leader's 5 questions for any AI-in-education pitch5 min read▶

Vendors, enthusiastic staff, board members with a demo they saw at a conference - the pitches are coming either way. Five questions sort them fast, and each maps to a session in this track:

  • 1 · "Show me the retention data, not the engagement data." Practice scores and usage minutes can rise while learning falls - the PNAS trap. Unassisted performance is the number that counts. (a2)
  • 2 · "What does the tool refuse to do?" A tutor with no guardrails is an answer machine with a nicer logo. Ask where it gives hints instead of answers, and who designed that. (a2, and b6 in the educator track)
  • 3 · "How would this handle a false accusation?" If the pitch includes AI detection, this question usually ends the meeting. (a3)
  • 4 · "Where does student data go, and under what agreement?" Consumer tools with no data agreement are a privacy incident on a timer. (a4)
  • 5 · "What happens for the students and teachers who do not get this?" The 13%-vs-25% guidance gap says AI benefits currently flow to the already-advantaged. Make the equity plan explicit or expect the gap to widen. (a4)

Print these. They fit on an index card, and they will survive every product cycle this decade.
