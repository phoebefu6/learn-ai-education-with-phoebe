Part 1 · covers TeachAI + Vanderbilt rollout patterns, Gallup benchmarks, the PNAS measurement lesson

The rollout sequence that works 15 min live

Every failed AI rollout we studied broke the same rule: something shipped before the thing it depended on. A tool before a policy means every student guesses the rules. A mandate before champions means the staffroom hears surveillance. A pilot before PD means the pilot measures confusion. The sequence is four gates, one per quarter: policy signed → champions trained → guardrailed pilot → measured scale. And there is one famous detour - buy a detector, skip the policy - that feels decisive in September and ends in a false accusation by spring.

What counts as a gate A gate is a date plus evidence, decided in advance: "policy published" means the signed document is live and every teacher has seen it, not "the committee met". If a gate slips, the quarter slips - the sequence never reorders itself to rescue the calendar.
LiveWho owns which gate4 min▶

Plans without owners are wishes. Assign each gate to a role on day one:

  • The board or leadership team signs Q1 - the 3-tier policy and the false-accusation protocol carry weight only when the top of the institution has put a name on them.
  • The head or dean runs the gates - one person decides "passed" or "slipped" per quarter, in public. Gate decisions by committee are how detours get rationalized.
  • Champions own Q2 - the PD wave is theirs to schedule and theirs to model; leadership funds it and gets out of the way.
  • The data or IT lead owns the agreements - no pilot starts in Q3 until the vendor paperwork from a4 and the a5 rubric is signed, and that person has the authority to say no.
LiveChampions before mandates5 min▶

The champions model is one decision and one rule:

  • One champion per department - a volunteer with credibility, not the most senior person and not the biggest enthusiast. They become the local answer to "is this allowed?" and "how do I start?", which is where rollouts are actually won.
  • They run the vendor-course ladder first - the a5 sequence, all the way to the ISTE rung. By the time anything is mandated, every department already contains someone who has done the work, has the artifacts, and can say "here is what it saved me" in their own subject's language.

Mandates issued before champions exist are heard as surveillance. The same mandate issued after champions exist is heard as catching up with colleagues.

LiveMeasure retention, never engagement5 min▶

Three numbers belong on the rollout dashboard, and one famous number does not:

  • Retention and learning outcomes - unassisted performance weeks later. This is the PNAS lesson: the vanilla-chatbot students' practice scores rose 48% while their unassisted exam scores fell about 17% below the no-AI group. Engagement was the lie; retention was the truth.
  • Teacher hours saved - benchmarked against Gallup's 5.9 hours per week for weekly AI users, about six working weeks a year. If your pilot's teachers are far below that, the tooling or the PD is wrong.
  • Zero false accusations - an explicit target, tracked like a safety metric. One documented false accusation costs more trust than the entire rollout builds.
Real world

The benchmark is national. Gallup's 2025 survey of 2,232 US public-school teachers: 60% used AI this school year, 32% weekly, and weekly users save about 5.9 hours a week - on preparing to teach (37%), worksheets (33%), and modifying materials (28%). Your Q4 readout gets to say how your pilot compares to those numbers, not to a vendor's slide.

Self-studyThe arithmetic of trust: why zero is the target3 min read▶

The zero-false-accusation target sounds soft until you run the numbers the way Vanderbilt did in 2023: a claimed 1% false-positive rate across roughly 75,000 papers a year meant about 750 falsely accused students annually - so they disabled Turnitin's AI detector, and Michigan State and Northwestern followed. Three implications for your dashboard:

  • Small rates times big volumes make headlines. Any per-essay error rate, multiplied by an institution's yearly volume, produces a steady stream of harmed students - concentrated, the Stanford evidence shows, on honest non-native writers.
  • One case outweighs the rollout. The trust a single false accusation burns - with the student, the family, the union, the local press - exceeds what a year of good pilots builds.
  • Zero is achievable - not because nothing will ever look suspicious, but because Part 2's protocol means no accusation is ever made on machine output alone.
Part 2 · covers the false-accusation protocol + change management for the staffroom

The protocol you write before the first incident 24 min workshop

Every school eventually faces the moment: a piece of work looks too polished, and someone wants to act. Institutions that improvise in that moment reach for a detector and manufacture a crisis. Institutions that wrote the protocol in advance handle it as due process. Write these five steps into policy now, while nobody is accused of anything, and have staff, students, and parents see them before they are ever needed:

1 · No detector output as evidence, ever. Detectors falsely flagged 61%+ of honest essays by non-native English speakers, OpenAI retired its own at a 26% catch rate, and Vanderbilt turned Turnitin's off after arithmetic showed roughly 750 falsely accused students a year at their scale.

2 · A conversation with the process trail. The teacher and student sit down with the portfolio - outline, drafts, AI transcript - and talk about how the work was made. Evidence, not vibes.

3 · A student walkthrough of their own work - the oral pattern. Someone who wrote it can explain a paragraph's choices in their own words; someone who outsourced it cannot. This is the strongest signal available and it is also teaching.

4 · Presumption of honesty absent evidence. If the trail and the walkthrough do not show a violation, the matter ends - explicitly and on the record. Honest students must be able to trust the process more than the accuser's hunch.

5 · Documented outcome + review. Every case, either way, is written up and reviewed - so the process improves and no student's file carries an undocumented shadow.

Where the pattern comes from The protocol assembles the published patterns: Vanderbilt's guidance after disabling its detector, the process-evidence and oral approaches from the higher-ed teaching centers, and the TeachAI toolkit's governance framing. Put it inside the 3-tier policy document from a3, and have every stakeholder sign the same page.
LiveMoving the "we've always done it this way" staffroom6 min▶

The change-management rule for skeptical staff is one sentence: start with time-savings wins, never lead with surveillance.

  • Open with the six weeks. Weekly AI users get back about six working weeks a year on prep, worksheets, and adapting materials. That is the first slide - a gift, not a demand. Let a champion show their own reclaimed Sunday.
  • Never open with integrity tooling. A rollout announced as "we are watching for AI cheating" recruits every teacher's students into an arms race and every teacher into being a cop. The protocol above exists precisely so integrity is handled by process, quietly.
  • Let refusal be free. Champions and volunteers first; the hold-outs join when the policy protects them and a colleague next door is visibly saving hours. Mandates, if ever, come last and smallest.

Real world

The vendors already voted for this order. Across the six major educator courses - Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, OpenAI, Common Sense, ISTE - every one teaches redesign and transparency, and not one endorses detection. When the companies selling the tools will not sell you the shortcut, the sequence above is not caution. It is the industry consensus.

Self-studyThe next 12 months: watch list and don't-fund list4 min read▶

What to watch - four moving fronts worth a quarterly check:

  • Guardrailed modes maturing - the Learning Mode / Khanmigo pattern spreading across vendors; expect "Socratic by default" to become a checkbox worth verifying, not trusting.
  • Under-13 walled gardens - the elementary-school gap between consumer terms (13+) and classroom reality is where the next product wave lands.
  • UNESCO framework adoption - as ministries and districts crosswalk PD to the 15 blocks, your a5 ladder mapping becomes reportable, not just internal.
  • Detection lawsuits - falsely accused students are reaching lawyers; every case strengthens the protocol-over-detector position you adopted tonight.

What NOT to fund - two lines that keep boards out of trouble:

  • AI detectors - the evidence from a3 has not changed: false flags concentrated on honest non-native writers, trivially evadable, retired by their own makers.
  • Unguarded chatbot licenses for students - raw access failed the only well-designed trial we have. If it does not pass the a5 rubric's first question, it does not get a purchase order.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The board wants momentum: license a student chatbot for September. What does the rollout playbook say ships first?

Policy before tools, champions before mandates, PD before pilots, evidence before scale. The detector-first shortcut is the red detour on tonight's timeline - it ends at the false-accusation dead end.

2 · Step 1 of the false-accusation protocol is:

Everything else in the protocol - the process-trail conversation, the student walkthrough, the presumption of honesty, the documented review - only works because step 1 removes the tool that falsely flagged 61%+ of honest non-native writers.

3 · Which metric belongs on the rollout dashboard?

The PNAS trial is the warning: practice engagement rose 48% while unassisted exam scores fell about 17%. Measure what remains when the AI is switched off, benchmark hours saved against Gallup's 5.9 h/wk, and track false accusations like a safety metric.
