The evidence, in plain English

Four studies carry almost everything a leader needs to know about AI and learning, and you can retell each one in a sentence. A randomized trial where the same AI either hurt exam scores by 17% or boosted practice by 127% - depending only on design. A Harvard trial where a designed tutor doubled learning. An MIT brain-scan study where chatbot writers could not quote their own essays. And a study of 319 knowledge workers where more confidence in AI meant less critical thinking. Tonight you get each study as a card, all four placed on one quadrant, and a live demo where you watch the harmful and the helpful version of the same tool play out on one student. Then the policy consequences: fund design, pilot with guardrails, and one question that sorts every vendor.

Part 1 · covers Bastani PNAS 2025 · Kestin 2025 · Kosmyna MIT 2025 · Lee CHI 2025

The four studies 15 min live

The evidence on AI and learning is genuinely two-sided, and both sides are strong. Unguarded chatbot access measurably harms retention even while practice soars. Well-designed AI tutoring measurably beats a good classroom. The variable is never the model - it is the design wrapped around it. One quadrant holds all four studies: unguarded vs designed on one axis, what you measure during practice vs what survives to retention on the other.

How to use the quadrant Every claim, pilot report, and vendor demo you meet from now on goes in one of these four cells. If someone can only show you evidence from the top row - practice, engagement, satisfaction - the bottom row is still an open question, and it is the row where the PNAS harm was hiding.
LiveStudy 1 · Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 - the two-sided trial4 min▶

One sentence: the same AI hurt or helped depending only on its design. A randomized controlled trial with about 1,000 high-school math students, published in PNAS - the strongest design in this space:

  • Vanilla chatbot arm: practice scores +48%. Then, on the unassisted exam, about 17% worse than students who had no AI at all. The chatbot did the work; the learning never happened.
  • Guardrailed tutor arm: the same underlying model, redesigned to give hints and never direct answers. Practice +127% - and the exam harm was eliminated.
Real world

The trap is that the harmed students felt great. If this trial had been evaluated the way most institutional pilots are - usage, satisfaction, practice performance - the vanilla chatbot would have been declared a triumph and rolled out to every classroom. The harm only appears when someone measures unassisted performance. Remember that when you read any pilot report.

LiveStudy 2 · Kestin et al., Scientific Reports 2025 - the upside3 min▶

One sentence: a well-designed AI tutor beat a good Harvard classroom - roughly double the learning, in less time.

  • A randomized trial in Harvard physics courses: students working with a carefully designed AI tutor learned more than twice as much in less time than students in an active-learning classroom - and active learning was already the gold standard being beaten.
  • The tutor was not a raw chatbot: it was built with pedagogy in it - structured guidance, no answer-dumping - the same design family as the PNAS guardrailed arm.

This is the study that keeps the conversation honest in the other direction. The lesson of PNAS is not "keep AI away from students." Designed well, this is the largest learning gain most leaders will see in their careers. The two studies together are the whole argument: fund the design.

Real world

Why this study matters at budget time. "Avoid harm" arguments fund caution; "double the learning" arguments fund programs. When you make the case for design work - guardrailed tutors, redesigned assessment, teacher training - Kestin is the number that turns it from an insurance policy into an investment.

LiveStudy 3 · Kosmyna et al., MIT 2025 - the brain-scan warning3 min▶

One sentence: under EEG, chatbot-assisted writers showed the weakest neural engagement and could not quote their own essays minutes after writing them.

  • MIT researchers put essay writers under EEG. The LLM-assisted group showed the weakest neural connectivity of all groups - and, most memorably, could not quote from their own essays shortly after finishing them. The words went through them without touching them.
  • The honesty rail, every time you cite it: this is a preprint, n=54. It has not passed peer review. Present it as suggestive and consistent with the PNAS result - never as settled fact. Leaders who add that caveat unprompted are more credible on every other number they quote.
LiveStudy 4 · Lee et al., CHI 2025 - the one about your staff3 min▶

One sentence: across 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in AI went with less critical thinking.

  • Microsoft and CMU researchers studied 319 knowledge workers using AI in real tasks (CHI 2025). The pattern: the more people trusted the AI, the less critically they thought - the work shifted from doing to verifying, and often the verifying quietly stopped.
  • This is the study that moves the problem out of the classroom. The answer-machine dynamic is not a teenager problem - it shows up in your staffroom, your admin office, and your own inbox. Whatever policy you set for students, plan a version of it for adults.

For L&D heads in the room: this is your bridge study. The same design question - does the AI create productive struggle or replace it - decides whether corporate training with AI builds capability or erodes it.

Self-studyHow to read an education study in five minutes4 min read▶

You do not need a research methods course - you need four questions, in order:

  • 1 · Was there a control group with random assignment? An RCT (PNAS, Kestin) can say "the AI caused this". A survey or observational study can only say "these things travel together". Weight them accordingly.
  • 2 · What was actually measured, and when? The whole PNAS story lives in this question: practice scores said triumph, the unassisted exam said harm. If a study (or vendor) only measured performance with the AI still on, it measured the tool, not the learning.
  • 3 · Peer-reviewed or preprint? PNAS, Scientific Reports, and CHI passed review. Kosmyna has not yet - which is why it gets the "suggestive, n=54" label every single time. Preprints are leads, not verdicts.
  • 4 · Who paid, and who benefits? Not a reason to dismiss - a reason to ask question 2 twice as hard when the answer is "the company selling the tool".

Five minutes, four questions, and you will read AI-in-education claims better than most of the people forwarding them to you.

Demo · the PNAS trial, replayed on one student

Watch the -17% and the +127% happen ★ 12 min · run it live

This is Marcus, from the Riverview unit your teachers work with in the educator track - a capable student, a persuasive essay due, and a chatbot open in another tab. Step through the moment twice. First with every lever off: the AI defaults to answer machine and you watch the -17% story happen one small, reasonable choice at a time. Then toggle AI roles on and run it again: the same student meets a designed tutor - hints, not answers - and you watch the +127% version of the same evening.

Run the trace with all levers off. Watch where Marcus stops thinking - and notice that no single step feels like cheating. This is the vanilla-chatbot arm of the PNAS trial: practice looks great, the exam bill arrives later.

Toggle AI roles on and step through again. The AI now scaffolds instead of solves - Marcus builds his own thesis against pushback. This is the guardrailed-tutor arm: +127% practice, exam harm gone.

Say the leader's version out loud: same student, same AI, same evening - one design decision apart. Nothing you can buy off the shelf made the difference; the design did.

Part 2 · from evidence to institutional policy

What this means for policy 12 min live

Four studies, one quadrant, one demo - and three policy moves fall straight out of them. None requires a new budget line as much as a new default: your institution stops evaluating tools and starts evaluating designs.

LiveThe three policy moves8 min▶
  • 1 · Fund design, not tools. The PNAS trial's two arms ran on the same model - the entire difference was design work: guardrails, pedagogy, assessment. Yet most budgets buy licenses and fund zero design hours. Flip the ratio: every AI line item should carry the design, training, and assessment-redesign work that makes it land on the right side of the quadrant.
  • 2 · Make "pilot with guardrails" the default motion. No raw-chatbot pilots, ever - the evidence says that arm is the harmful one. Every pilot names its guardrail design up front and its retention measure (unassisted performance, not usage) before it starts. A pilot that cannot say how it will measure retention is an engagement experiment, not a learning one.
  • 3 · Ask every vendor the Discernment question: "Show me the retention data, not the engagement data." Engagement dashboards would have crowned the vanilla chatbot. Vendors with real learning products can answer; vendors with answer machines change the subject. One sentence, and it sorts the whole market for you.
Real world

Run the test on the demo you just watched. Both versions of Marcus's evening would light up an engagement dashboard - he used the tool heavily in each. Only a retention measure separates them. That is why move 2 and move 3 are the same idea wearing different clothes: measure what survives when the AI is closed.

LiveHandling the pushback in the room4 min▶

Three objections you will meet when you bring this evidence to your leadership team, and the honest answers:

  • "One study of Turkish math students - does it generalize?" Fair instinct, and the reason tonight has four studies, not one: an RCT (PNAS), a second RCT at Harvard (Kestin), a physiological signal (MIT, preprint - say so), and a workplace study (CHI). Different populations, methods, and settings, all pointing the same way.
  • "So we should ban student AI use?" The evidence does not say that - the designed arms are the biggest learning gains on record. It says unguarded defaults are harmful, so the ban-vs-allow framing is the wrong question. Design-vs-no-design is the question.
  • "Our satisfaction scores for the pilot are excellent." So were the vanilla chatbot's practice scores - +48%. "Feels like learning" is the documented failure mode, not evidence. Ask what the unassisted measure shows.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the PNAS trial, the vanilla chatbot group hit +48% in practice but -17% on the unassisted exam. What did the guardrailed tutor - the same base model - change?

Hints instead of answers: practice +127% with no exam harm. Same AI, opposite quadrant - which is why the policy unit is the design, not the tool.

2 · Why does "the students love it and use it constantly" fail as evidence that a tool helps learning?

"Feels like learning" is the documented failure mode. The vanilla-chatbot students were the happy ones right up until the exam. Only an unassisted retention measure separates the two arms.

3 · A vendor demo shows daily active students, session lengths, and glowing satisfaction scores. What is your Discernment question?

Engagement dashboards would have crowned the harmful arm of the PNAS trial. Retention - unassisted performance - is the number that separates learning products from answer machines. (And if the pitch touts a detector, a3 is your next session.)
