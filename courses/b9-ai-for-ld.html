Part 1 · covers Bersin's maturity model + the ATD and LinkedIn Learning L&D curricula

The corporate mirror: where L&D actually stands 8 min live

Corporate learning is a $400B global spend - more than $1,400 per employee per year - and Josh Bersin's Corporate Learning Maturity Model says most of it is stuck at the bottom. Four levels: Static Training (scheduled courses, one size fits all - roughly 33% of the market sits here), Scaled Learning (the digital-library era), Integrated Development (learning in the flow of work), and Dynamic Enablement (learning fused with work itself). The stakes are not cosmetic: Level-4 organizations are 10x more likely to be innovation leaders and 6x more likely to exceed financial targets. AI is the engine that moves orgs up this staircase - and your classroom levers are how it moves them safely.

LiveWhat L&D teams actually do with AI5 min▶

From the verified ATD and LinkedIn Learning curricula, six workloads keep showing up - notice how each one is a Riverview move wearing a suit:

  • Skills-gap analysis - what can this workforce do versus what the strategy needs. The enterprise version of knowing your class before you plan the unit.
  • Per-role skill maps - the objectives layer (b4), written per job instead of per grade.
  • Hyper-personalized learning paths - differentiation (b4) at 10,000-employee scale.
  • AI-assisted content creation - first drafts by AI, designer QC. Your worksheet workflow from b2, industrialized.
  • Performance support - help in the flow of work. The guardrailed tutor's corporate cousin (more in Part 2).
  • Content rationalization - AI audits the sprawling course library for overlap and rot. No classroom equivalent; enterprises hoard content the way nobody's classroom can.
LiveReading the staircase without flattering yourself3 min▶

Every org places itself one level too high. The honest diagnostic is not "what do we aspire to" but "what happens on a random Tuesday":

  • Level 1 test - if learning mostly means "a course was scheduled," you are at Static Training, with a third of the market. No shame; that is the baseline.
  • Level 2 test - a big library and completion tracking, but the same library for everyone? Scaled Learning.
  • Level 3 test - does learning show up inside the work tools, mapped to the role? That is Integrated Development, and it is where per-role skill maps start paying rent.
  • Level 4 test - does the system notice a skills gap before the manager does, and route support in the flow of work? Dynamic Enablement.

The level you are AT decides which AI capability to buy next - buying level-4 tools for a level-1 org is the enterprise version of handing a vanilla chatbot to a Grade 9 class.

LiveThe CHI 2025 evidence: why the enterprise needs your levers5 min▶

The study that makes this session urgent is not about students at all. Lee et al. at CHI 2025 (Microsoft/CMU) surveyed 319 knowledge workers and found a clean, uncomfortable pattern: the more confident people were in the AI, the less critical thinking they applied - and their work shifted from doing to verifying.

That is the PNAS answer-machine effect, relocated to the office. And it hands L&D a design brief nobody had five years ago: training must teach verification as a skill - how to check an AI draft, when to distrust a confident answer, what "good" looks like without the tool. The walkthrough demo and the capability conversation exist precisely because completion certificates cannot see whether verification skill exists.

Real world

The compliance-training test case. An L&D team rolls out an AI assistant to speed up compliance scenarios, and pass rates jump - the practice-score mirage from b1, in corporate clothing. The fix is the same fix: a work sample without the tool, a walkthrough with it, and a five-minute capability conversation. If the skill only exists when the bot is open, the training did not work - it just felt like it did.

LiveBuild-along: translate one training module8 min▶

Pick any training module you know - onboarding, product knowledge, a compliance refresher - and run the six rows on it, out loud, with a partner:

  • Policy - which AI uses are allowed for this role, on this task, with this tool?
  • Simulator - what does the learner practice AGAINST the AI, before the real thing?
  • Support bot - where does a coach-not-doer bot live in the flow of work?
  • Work sample + walkthrough - what do they show you without the tool, then with it?
  • Capability conversation - what three questions would expose an outsourced skill?
  • Data discipline - what employee data must never leave vetted systems?

Six answers on one page is a serious first draft of an enterprise learning design - most L&D consultancies would charge for the same page.

Self-studyThree traps when the classroom moves to the office3 min read▶

The translation table works, but three enterprise habits will try to bend it back:

  • Tool-first rollouts. The enterprise buys the AI platform, then asks what to do with it - the corporate version of handing out chatbots before the policy exists. The table runs policy first for the same reason your unit does.
  • Compliance theater. A mandatory module with a completion checkbox is the enterprise "polished final draft": it proves attendance, not capability. The walkthrough demo is the antidote, and it costs five minutes.
  • Dashboard worship. Completion rates climb, so the program is declared a success - the practice-score mirage from the PNAS study, at org scale. If the dashboard cannot show capability without the tool, it is measuring the wrong thing.

Name the trap out loud in the meeting and you will usually be the only person in the room who can - that is what eight sessions of classroom evidence buys you.

Part 3 · the instructional designer's lens

ADGIE and the measurement shift ◐ self-study

Two shorter ideas complete the enterprise picture - read them this week, before the capstone.

Self-studyADGIE, applied to corporate content3 min read▶

b4 introduced ADGIE - ADDIE with AI in every phase - for lesson design. It was born for corporate work, and it maps directly:

  • Analysis - AI personas and skill maps stand in for early learner research.
  • Design - AI prototypes the training plan; the designer chooses, not types.
  • Generation - AI writes first drafts of modules and scenarios; the designer QCs every claim.
  • Individualisation - the phase classic ADDIE never had: real-time adaptation per learner.
  • Evaluation - continuous, not end-of-course.

The rule from b4 still holds: AI accelerates every phase, and the designer owns every decision.

Where ADGIE earns its keep in the enterprise Content rationalization - the workload with no classroom equivalent - is Analysis and Evaluation run against the existing library instead of new learners: AI maps what the courses claim to teach, the skill maps say what roles actually need, and the gap between the two is the rationalization report.
Self-studyMeasure capability demonstrated, not courses completed2 min read▶

Your classroom mantra since b5 has been "grade the thinking, not the text." The enterprise version: measure capability demonstrated, not courses completed. Completion dashboards are the corporate equivalent of engagement metrics - they measure attendance at learning, not learning. A Level-4 org in Bersin's model measures whether the skill shows up in work: the work sample, the walkthrough, the capability conversation. If your L&D dashboard cannot tell you who can do the thing without the tool, it is measuring the wrong thing - the enterprise version of "retention, not engagement."

2 · What is the enterprise translation of Riverview's process portfolio + oral defense?
