The loop, and the anatomy of a teaching prompt 15 min live

The ONE skill all six vendor courses teach: prompting is not a sentence, it is a cycle. Google's course frames it exactly - write a prompt, iterate to refine it, evaluate the output, prepare to incorporate it into your work. And the write step has an anatomy. A strong teaching prompt carries five layers: role + class context + constraint + format + example - "You are helping a Grade 9 teacher... 28 students, mixed ability... never write full paragraphs students could submit... output as a table." Miss a layer and the model fills the gap with the average classroom on the internet, which is not your classroom.

LiveThe loop: write, iterate, evaluate, incorporate5 min▶

Four moves, run as a cycle - and notice how neatly they sit inside b1's 4D loop:

  • Write - draft the prompt with all five anatomy layers. This is Description from the 4D loop: can you say what you actually want?
  • Iterate to refine - draft 1 is a first offer, not a verdict. Tell the model what is generic and anchor it to YOUR class. One good iterate beats five prompt rewrites from scratch.
  • Evaluate the output - this is Discernment: is it accurate, does it fit these 28 students, would it survive Ms. Tan reading it aloud?
  • Prepare to incorporate - the output becomes YOUR material: edited, owned, and never forwarded raw. That handoff is where Diligence starts.

New users write once and accept. Fluent educators loop two or three times in the same minutes - the loop is fast, it just is not optional.

LiveThe five layers, one at a time5 min▶

Each layer closes a specific failure mode:

  • Role - "You are helping a Grade 9 teacher" sets the audience and the register. Without it you get advice written for nobody.
  • Class context - "28 students, mixed ability, three IEP writing goals, the plastics-ban unit." The single highest-value layer, because it is the one thing the model cannot guess.
  • Constraint - what the AI must NOT do: "never write full paragraphs students could submit." Constraints do more work in teaching prompts than in any other domain, because the failure mode is not a bad draft - it is a draft that quietly does the students' thinking.
  • Format - "output as a table" turns a wall of text into something you can scan at 7am.
  • Example - one exemplar row or sentence to match. Cheapest way to transmit taste.
Context + constraint carry the weight If you only remember two layers, keep those two. Context makes the output fit your room; the constraint keeps it on the right side of the learning. A long prompt with neither is still a weak prompt.

The loop and the anatomy are the shared core - here is what each vendor adds on top, so you know where to go for the delta:

The five prompts every teacher needs ★ 24 min · build along

Five tasks, five prompts, all on Ms. Tan's unit. Each comes as a weak/strong pair plus the iterate move - the exact sentence to say when draft 1 comes back generic. Adapt the strong versions to your own class tonight; the anatomy transfers even when the subject does not.

Real world

These are not hypothetical tasks. Gallup's 2025 survey of US teachers found the top three things teachers already use AI for are preparing to teach (37%), creating worksheets and assignments (33%), and modifying materials to fit student needs (28%) - exactly the ground prompts 1-3 cover. The five prompts tonight are the highest-traffic lanes of the six-weeks-a-year saving from b1.

LivePrompt 1 · differentiation - the Mollick pattern6 min▶

Mollick's signature pattern: context-rich, and the AI asks YOU questions before it drafts. You know things about your class the model never will - the pattern makes it collect them first.

Weak - what most teachers typeDifferentiate this lesson for my weaker students.
Strong - context-rich, asks you firstYou are an experienced teaching assistant helping a Grade 9 teacher differentiate one lesson. Class context: 28 students, mixed ability - several strong writers, a few reading below grade level, three students with IEP writing goals. The lesson: drafting body paragraphs for a persuasive essay on "Should our city ban single-use plastics?" Before you suggest anything, ask me up to five questions about my class that would change your recommendations. Wait for my answers. Then propose three tiered versions of the practice task. Constraint: never write full paragraphs students could submit - scaffolds, sentence starters, and success criteria only. Output as a table: tier / who it serves / the task / the scaffold.

The iterate move. Draft still generic after the questions? Say: "Too generic - tie every suggestion to the plastics-ban essay and name which of my three groups each tier serves."

LivePrompt 2 · the lesson-plan variant5 min▶

Not "write a lesson plan" - a variant of YOUR plan, under your constraints, in a format you can scan before first period.

WeakWrite a lesson plan on persuasive writing.
Strong - your objective, your constraintsYou are helping a Grade 9 teacher plan one 55-minute lesson inside a persuasive-essay unit ("Should our city ban single-use plastics?"). 28 students, mixed ability. Today's objective: every student connects one claim to one piece of evidence in their own words. Constraints: at least 20 minutes of student writing time; no activity that needs devices; nothing I could not photocopy tonight. Output as a table: minutes / teacher does / students do / what I watch for.

The iterate move. First drafts overstuff the hour. Say: "Cut this to the two activities that best serve the objective, and give the reclaimed minutes to student writing."

LivePrompt 3 · the rubric first draft5 min▶

AI is excellent at rubric SCAFFOLDING and mediocre at rubric JUDGMENT - so the prompt bakes in whose judgment is final.

WeakMake a rubric for a persuasive essay.
Strong - a draft that knows it is a draftDraft a FIRST-DRAFT rubric for a Grade 9 persuasive essay on the single-use plastics ban. I will revise every cell, so label it DRAFT. Four criteria: claim, evidence, reasoning, conventions. Four levels each. Constraints: describe observable evidence in the essay, not vague adjectives; use language a Grade 9 student can read without a dictionary; no level can be reached through polished grammar alone. Output as a table: criterion / level 1 / level 2 / level 3 / level 4.

The iterate move. The classic failure: "Levels 3 and 4 read the same. Rewrite them so the difference is something I could point to in the essay itself."

LivePrompt 4 · the parent-email draft4 min▶

The highest-stakes writing teachers do all week - which is exactly why the prompt states, in writing, that nothing goes out unreviewed.

WeakWrite an email to a parent about their kid missing homework.
Strong - facts only, you press sendDraft an email for me to REVIEW and send myself - it goes nowhere until I have edited it. Recipient: the parent of a Grade 9 student who has missed three essay checkpoints this unit. Tone: warm, factual, on the same team as the parent. Constraints: state only the facts I gave you; no speculation about causes; no diagnosis language; under 150 words; end with one specific question inviting the parent's view. Leave a [NAME] placeholder - I add personal details myself, never in this chat.

The iterate move. "Warmer opening, and swap the question - ask what support looks like at home, not why the work is late." (Why the placeholder rule matters becomes b8's whole session.)

LivePrompt 5 · the exit-ticket generator4 min▶

The fastest daily win: three questions aligned to today's objective, with wrong answers that tell you something.

WeakMake quiz questions about persuasive essays.
Strong - objective-aligned, misconception-awareGenerate a 3-question exit ticket for today's Grade 9 lesson. Objective: students connect one claim to one piece of evidence in their own words. Mix: one "explain in your own words", one "spot the weak evidence" whose wrong option is a plausible misconception, one "apply it to your own plastics-ban essay". Constraints: answerable in five minutes on paper; no trick wording. Output: the three numbered questions, then a teacher-only answer key noting which misconception each wrong answer reveals.

The iterate move. "Question 1 is pure recall. Rewrite it so a student cannot answer without actually connecting a claim to evidence."

Part 3 · self-study

The meta-lesson: starters, not spells

Self-studyMollick's meta-lesson: structured conversation starters3 min read▶

Mollick's teaching on prompts lands on one reframe: they are "structured conversation starters," not magic spells. Nobody hands you the perfect incantation, and collecting other people's prompts is a weak strategy - because the value is not in the wording, it is in the anatomy plus the conversation that follows. That is why every prompt tonight shipped with an iterate move: the second message you send matters as much as the first. Learn the five layers and you can rebuild any prompt on this page from memory for any subject you teach.

Self-studyThe fluency stance: you review everything3 min read▶

This is Diligence from b1's 4D loop, applied to your own desk: you review everything, AI drafts nothing final. The rubric is a DRAFT until you revise it. The parent email goes nowhere until you edit and send it yourself. The exit ticket gets read before it gets photocopied. Notice the constraints tonight all pointed the same direction - never full paragraphs students could submit, no speculation in the email, no level passed on grammar alone. The never-submit-raw rule you will set for students in b7 starts as a rule you model yourself. Teachers who forward raw AI output are teaching a lesson too - just not the one on the board.

Self-studyBuild a prompt shelf, not a prompt pile2 min read▶

Tonight's five prompts want a home. Two habits make them compound:

  • Save the SKELETON, not the transcript - keep each prompt with its class-context slot blank, so next term's 28 students drop straight in. A prompt with someone else's class baked in is single-use.
  • Save the iterate moves with the prompt - the second message is half the craft. When a colleague asks for "your rubric prompt," hand over both lines or you have handed over half a tool.

Five well-kept skeletons beat a folder of two hundred copied prompts - and in b4 these five become the building blocks of a full unit plan.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 20-30 min total

  • Run prompt 1 (differentiation) on a real lesson of yours. Let the AI ask its five questions and answer honestly - then note which question you could not have thought to volunteer.
  • Take your weakest recent prompt and rebuild it with all five layers: role, class context, constraint, format, example. Compare outputs side by side.
  • Practice one iterate move per day: when a draft is generic, name what is generic and anchor it to your class instead of starting over.
  • Write your personal never-submit-raw line - the sentence you would say if a colleague asked whether you "just use ChatGPT for" your rubrics - and keep it honest.
