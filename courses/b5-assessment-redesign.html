Assessment redesign

This is the session the whole course funnels into. The question "did AI write this?" is statistically unanswerable - OpenAI's own detector caught 26% and got retired; detectors falsely flag 61% of honest non-native writers. The question "can this student do the thing?" is completely answerable - you just have to design the assessment so the answer is visible. Tonight you rebuild Riverview's essay unit around process evidence: portfolios, in-class components, oral defenses, and rubrics that grade thinking with AI in the room. The detector goes in the bin, and nothing of value is lost.

Part 1 · covers the detection evidence base: Liang et al., OpenAI, Vanderbilt

The wrong question and the right one 7 min live

"Did AI touch this text?" sounds like a question a tool could answer. The verified record says no tool can: OpenAI retired its own detector in July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy" - it caught 26% of AI text and falsely flagged 9% of human text. The Stanford team behind the Patterns study found detectors flagged 61%+ of honest TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers - while being trivially evadable by anyone who asked the AI to "elevate" its own prose. Vanderbilt did the institutional arithmetic and turned Turnitin's detector off: a claimed 1% false-positive rate across ~75,000 papers a year means ~750 falsely accused students, every year.

LiveWhy every vendor landed in the same place4 min▶

We verified six major vendor courses for this track - Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, OpenAI, Common Sense, ISTE. Not one endorses AI detection. OpenAI's educator FAQ says it plainly: detectors have not "proven to reliably distinguish between AI-generated and human-generated content" - their own tests flagged Shakespeare and the Declaration of Independence. The converged industry position is exactly this session: transparency norms + human judgment + assessment redesign.

Real world

The accusation IS the punishment. The Washington Post tested detectors on real student essays and watched an innocent high-schooler get flagged. A student facing a false AI accusation cannot prove a negative - there is no exculpatory evidence for "I just wrote it." That asymmetry is why Vanderbilt, Michigan State, and Northwestern switched detection off rather than tune it. Your redesigned unit never puts a student in that position: the evidence exists BEFORE anyone is suspicious.

Self-studyBut my colleague swears the detector works3 min read▶

Three honest replies for the staffroom:

  • "It catches some cheaters." True - and it also accuses honest students at rates you cannot ethically accept. At 61% false flags for non-native writers, the tool is biased against exactly the students with the least power to push back.
  • "Turnitin claims under 1% false positives." Document-level, for high-scoring documents - Turnitin itself admitted ~4% at sentence level and lower reliability below its thresholds. And 1% of a whole school is still hundreds of accusations.
  • "So students just get away with it?" No - the redesigned unit catches outsourced work BETTER than a detector, because in-class writing and oral defenses test the student, not the text. The two outsourcers at Riverview get caught by evidence, not vibes, in Demo 2.
Part 2 · covers Mollick "Building AI Exercises", ISTE assessment integration, Vanderbilt course design

The redesign toolkit: four moves 8 min live

Every AI-resilient assessment in the wild is some mix of four moves. None of them ban AI - they make learning visible with AI in the room. That is the ISTE deep-dive framing ("assessment integration while maintaining academic integrity") and the practical heart of Mollick's Wharton course.

LiveMove 1 + 2: process portfolios and in-class anchors5 min▶

Move 1 - the process portfolio. The unit grade attaches to the trail, not just the destination: outline → draft 1 → AI transcript (if used) → draft 2 → final. Each artifact is small; together they make the thinking visible. A student who outsourced the essay has no trail - and producing a fake trail is more work than writing the essay.

Move 2 - the in-class anchor. One timed, device-free paragraph per unit, on a familiar prompt. It is not a gotcha - it is a baseline. When a final draft towers over a student's in-class writing, that is a conversation, not an accusation. When they match, the polished draft is earned.

Effort budget The portfolio is NOT more grading. Grade the final draft as before; the trail is checked only when something needs explaining - like receipts. Ms. Tan's version: 4 checkpoints, 90 seconds each to skim, only 5 of 28 ever needed a second look.
LiveMove 3 + 4: oral defenses and AI-transparent rubrics5 min▶

Move 3 - the oral defense. Five minutes, three questions: "Walk me through your strongest argument. What did you reject? Defend your weakest citation." A student who did the thinking passes easily - it is a proud moment, not an interrogation. A student who outsourced it cannot survive question one. This is where Aiden's hidden gap surfaces in week 3 instead of at the exam.

Move 4 - the AI-transparent rubric. Add one visible row to your existing rubric: "AI use - disclosed, within tier, and the human thinking is evident." Harvard requires every syllabus to state an AI policy; Stanford tells students to cite AI like any tool. The rubric row makes disclosure normal and gradable instead of confessional.

★ The four moves, one line each Portfolio = the trail is the proof · In-class anchor = a baseline, not a trap · Oral defense = test the student, not the text · Transparent rubric = disclosure is a graded skill, not a confession.
Self-studyRedesign patterns by subject4 min read▶

The four moves wear different clothes per subject:

  • Math / sciences: the anchor is board work or a lab notebook; the "portfolio" is showing method variants; the oral is "explain why step 3 is legal."
  • Humanities: exactly the Riverview unit - drafts, transcripts, seminar defense.
  • Languages: in-class anchors dominate (production under time); AI becomes the conversation partner (b3's simulator role), never the translator.
  • Corporate L&D (b9 preview): the "oral defense" is a walkthrough demo - "show me how you'd do it without the tool, then with it."

Vanderbilt's course-design guidance adds the flipped variant: give students an AI-generated essay and grade their CRITIQUE of it - the AI output becomes the exam paper.

Real world

The redesigned Riverview unit, on one page. Week 1: policy tiers + outline (portfolio starts). Week 2: draft 1 + in-class anchor paragraph. Week 3: AI-assisted revision within tiers, transcript filed + 5-minute orals begin. Week 4: final draft + transparent-rubric grading. Total new teacher time: the orals (28 x 5 min, replacing one grading pass) and 90-second portfolio skims. Total detector spend: zero.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why did Vanderbilt disable Turnitin's AI detector?

The institutional arithmetic: low error RATES become large accusation COUNTS at scale - and a falsely accused student cannot prove a negative. Michigan State and Northwestern reached the same conclusion.

2 · A final draft towers over a student's in-class anchor writing. The redesigned response is:

The anchor creates a baseline, the portfolio holds the receipts, the oral tests the student rather than the text. Suspicion becomes a structured conversation with evidence - in both directions.

3 · What is the AI-transparent rubric row for?

The row grades "disclosed, within tier, human thinking evident." Jae's voluntary disclosure becomes the expected path - which is what Harvard's mandatory syllabus policies and Stanford's cite-your-AI guidance are engineering at scale.
