Part 1 · covers Anthropic AI Fluency (4D framework) + AI Fluency for Educators

The 4D loop: how fluent educators use AI 7 min live

Anthropic's AI Fluency framework - built with academics and taught in its educator course - names four competencies, and they run as a loop, not a checklist. Delegation: should AI touch this task at all, and which part? Description: can you tell it what you actually want? Discernment: can you judge what came back? Diligence: do you take responsibility - integrity, privacy, accuracy, and being transparent about AI's role? Every classroom decision in this course is one of these four questions wearing a costume.

LiveThe four levers of this course4 min▶

The 4D loop is how you think. The four levers are what your classroom needs so learning survives AI - and they are the four ways real classrooms fail:

  • Policy - without a clear, shared AI policy, every student guesses and every teacher improvises. Honesty becomes a confession.
  • AI roles - without scaffolded roles, the AI defaults to answer machine, and students submit words they cannot defend.
  • Redesigned assessment - without process evidence, a polished final draft is a black box with a name on it.
  • Privacy rails - without them, one helpful instinct pastes a student's protected record into a consumer chatbot.

There is a fifth toggle in the simulator - an AI detector. It is not a lever. You will discover why yourself in Demo 2, and get the full evidence in b5 and b7.

Real world

The six-weeks number is real. Gallup's 2025 survey of 2,232 US public-school teachers: 60% used AI this school year, and weekly users save about 5.9 hours a week - roughly six working weeks a year - on prep, worksheets, and adapting materials. The time saving is not hype. What happens to learning is the open question, and it is the one this course answers.

Self-studyDelegation: the question most educators skip3 min read▶

The fluency course's sharpest idea is that the first question is not "how do I prompt this?" but "should AI touch this task, and which part?" A quick delegation rule for teaching work:

  • Delegate freely - low-stakes, teacher-reviewed output: newsletter drafts, worksheet variants, differentiation ideas, rubric first drafts.
  • Delegate with design - anything student-facing: only through a scaffolded role (b3) or a guardrailed tutor (b6), never raw.
  • Do not delegate - the judgment that IS the job: grades, feedback that carries your relationship with the student, IEP decisions, anything requiring protected data (b8).

Write these three buckets on one sticky note and you have a working AI policy for your own desk - the classroom-wide version arrives in b7.

Part 2 · covers the evidence spine: Bastani et al. PNAS 2025, Kestin et al. 2025

Same tool, two classrooms 6 min live

The best-designed study in this space (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025) put ~1,000 high-school math students in a randomized trial. Students with a vanilla chatbot looked great in practice (+48%) - then scored about 17% worse than the no-AI group on the unassisted exam. Students with a guardrailed tutor - same underlying model, but designed to give hints and never direct answers - practiced far better (+127%) with no exam harm. And a Harvard trial found a well-designed AI tutor doubled learning versus an active classroom. Same technology. Opposite outcomes. Design decides.

LiveWhy "feels like learning" lies4 min▶

The trap in the PNAS study is that the harmed students felt great - their practice scores soared while the learning quietly stopped happening. Two more studies sharpen the picture:

  • MIT's EEG essay study (2025, preprint - suggestive, not conclusive): the chatbot group showed the weakest neural engagement and could not quote their own essays minutes after writing them.
  • Microsoft/CMU at CHI 2025 (319 knowledge workers): the more people trusted the AI, the less critically they thought - the work shifted from doing to verifying. That one is about YOUR staffroom, not just students.

The takeaway is not "AI harms learning." It is that fluency without friction is the failure mode - and every lever in this course is a way of putting the right friction back.

Self-studyWhat the vendor courses agree on3 min read▶

We verified the current syllabi of six major AI-for-educators offerings - Google's Gemini course, Microsoft's educator path, Anthropic's fluency courses, OpenAI's teacher foundations, Common Sense Education, and ISTE's deep dives. Three things they all converge on:

  • The prompt-iterate-evaluate loop as the core teacher skill (b2 teaches it once, properly).
  • Teacher time-saving first - planning, differentiation, materials, parent comms - before any student-facing use.
  • Redesign and transparency over detection. Not one of the six endorses AI detectors. OpenAI is on record: they don't reliably work.

Real world

That detector result is not a gimmick. Detectors falsely flagged 61% of honest TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers (Stanford, published in Patterns). OpenAI retired its own detector at a 26% catch rate. Vanderbilt did the arithmetic - roughly 750 falsely accused students a year at their scale - and turned Turnitin's detector off. The simulator's fifth toggle behaves the way the research says detectors behave.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the PNAS trial, students with vanilla chatbot access improved practice scores by 48%. What happened on the unassisted exam?

The answer-machine effect: practice looked great while learning stopped. The guardrailed-tutor group kept the practice gains WITHOUT the exam harm - same AI, different design.

2 · A teacher wonders whether to let AI draft feedback on student essays. Which 4D question comes FIRST?

Fluency starts before the prompt box. Feedback that carries your relationship with a student sits in the "do not delegate" bucket for most teachers - and that is a Delegation call, made first.

3 · At 12/12 on the scorecard, turning ON the AI detector drops the score to 11/12. Why?

Detectors flagged 61%+ of honest non-native TOEFL essays in the Stanford study. The two real cases in the unit were already caught by process evidence - the detector's only unique contribution was a false accusation.
