Part 1 · covers Vanderbilt, Harvard, Stanford policy patterns

The 3-tier pattern every institution converged on 7 min live

Ask three of the most-cited teaching centers in the US how to write an AI policy and you get one answer in three accents. Vanderbilt's exact tiers: "Generative AI use is allowed" / "allowed only in certain scenarios" / "never allowed." Harvard's version of the same ladder: maximally restrictive / mixed / fully-encouraging - and Harvard FAS REQUIRES every course to state one. Stanford adds the two rules that make any statement actually work: it must be self-contained (students do not know campus policy - your syllabus is the whole rulebook they will read), and students cite AI use like any other source. One course-level tier, then per-assignment overrides. That is the entire pattern.

LiveThree institutions, one pattern - the exact language5 min▶

The tier is only half the statement. Each tier carries its own duty of care - this is the part most homemade policies skip:

  • "Generative AI use is allowed" - Vanderbilt still asks you to state WHICH activities are allowed and what your disclosure policy is. "Allowed" without a disclosure norm is how honest students end up guessing.
  • "Allowed only in certain scenarios" - the workhorse tier for most courses. Vanderbilt's guidance: reiterate it in the LMS, not just the syllabus, and give detailed citation guidelines so "cite your AI use" has an actual format.
  • "Never allowed" - state the prohibition AND the consequences. A ban with no stated consequence is a bluff, and students call bluffs.

Harvard's naming - maximally restrictive / mixed / fully-encouraging - maps one-to-one onto the same ladder, and the FAS requirement means "I never said anything about AI" is no longer a position a Harvard course can hold. Stanford closes the loop: because students take five courses with five different policies, YOUR statement must stand alone, and AI gets cited like any source.

Part 2 · build-along

Write the Riverview statement, tier by tier 10 min live

Ms. Tan's course-level call: tier 2 - allowed only in certain scenarios - because the essay unit is where the learning lives. Then three per-assignment overrides, one per tier, so every assignment sheet answers the question before a student has to ask it.

LiveThe course-level statement, written live4 min▶

Write it in student language, not policy language. The Riverview version, which you will adapt for homework:

Riverview · course-level statement · tier 2AI use in this class: allowed only in certain scenarios. You may use AI to brainstorm, to get feedback on writing you did yourself, and to polish grammar. You may not use AI to write paragraphs or sections for you. Each assignment sheet states its own tier - the assignment tier always wins. If you used AI, say where and how on your portfolio's disclosure line, and cite it like any source. This statement lives in the syllabus and in the LMS. We read it together in week 1 and again before every major assignment.

Notice what makes it Stanford-proof: it is self-contained (no "see campus policy"), it names the citation habit, and it points at the disclosure line so honesty has a place to live.

LivePer-assignment overrides: one line on every assignment sheet3 min▶

The course tier sets the default; the assignment sheet gets the final word. Riverview's three overrides:

AssignmentTierThe line on the sheet
Brainstorming + research notesTier 1 · allowed"Use AI freely to explore. Note in your outline which ideas you kept."
Essay unit (drafts + final)Tier 2 · certain scenarios"AI for feedback and polish on your own drafts only. File the transcript in your portfolio and fill in the disclosure line."
In-class anchor paragraphTier 3 · never"Timed, device-free. This is your baseline - it protects you."

The override line takes ten seconds to add and removes the single biggest integrity failure: students genuinely not knowing which rules apply to WHICH piece of work.

Effort budget The whole policy build is one page and three sentences: a 120-word course statement written once, plus a one-line override per assignment sheet. Compare that to the hours one integrity case costs - policy is the cheapest lever in the course by an order of magnitude.
LiveDisclosure norms + the transparency habits3 min▶

A policy nobody remembers is a policy nobody follows. Two pieces make disclosure real:

  • The rubric row from b5 - "AI use: disclosed, within tier, and the human thinking is evident." Disclosure is now a graded skill with a place on the page. When Jae volunteers "I used AI to tighten my intro, transcript attached," that is the expected path working - a normal moment, not a confession extracted under pressure.
  • Vanderbilt's transparency habits - the policy lives in the syllabus AND the LMS, gets said aloud at term start, and gets a reminder before every major assignment. Written-once-mentioned-never is how policies die.
★ The whole session in one line Pick a tier, write it self-contained, override per assignment, grade the disclosure, say it out loud - three times a term minimum.
Self-studyThe tiers in other subjects3 min read▶

The ladder is subject-agnostic; only the override lines change clothes:

  • Math / sciences: problem sets often run tier 2 ("AI to check your method, never to produce the working"); board work and lab practicals are natural tier 3; exploring a new topic before class is tier 1.
  • Languages: production under time is tier 3 almost by definition; AI as conversation partner (b3's simulator role) is a clean tier 2 with a transcript; vocabulary drilling sits happily in tier 1.
  • Corporate L&D (b9 preview): the same three tiers, renamed for adults - open use with disclosure, tool-assisted with a walkthrough demo, and unassisted certification checkpoints.

Whatever the subject, the failure mode is identical: a course where students must GUESS the tier per task. The override line exists to delete the guessing.

Part 3 · handling suspicion without detectors

When a draft still looks wrong 8 min live

Policy plus redesign prevents most integrity problems - not all. When a final draft towers over everything you know about a student's writing, you need a process that starts from a presumption of honesty and never touches a detector score. Here it is.

LiveThe conversation script + the process-trail review4 min▶

The opening line, word for word: "Your final is much stronger than your in-class work - walk me through how you got there." It is a genuine question with two honest exits: the student who did the work walks you through it proudly, and the student who did not cannot survive the walk-through. No accusation was made either way.

Then the trail, together: the outline, the two drafts, the AI transcript, the in-class anchor. The b5 portfolio means the evidence already exists BEFORE anyone was suspicious - you are reading receipts, not building a case. Improvement with a visible trail is called learning; a leap with no trail is a conversation that continues.

LiveThe false-accusation protocol4 min▶

Leaders get the institutional version in a6; teachers run the classroom half. Five commitments:

  • Presume honesty. The trail speaks first, and most towering drafts have an innocent explanation - a tutor, a weekend, a topic the student loves.
  • Conversation, not accusation. The script above, in private, with curiosity in your voice.
  • Review the trail together. The student narrates their own portfolio - it is their evidence, in both directions.
  • Offer a demonstration, not a trial. If a gap remains: rewrite one section live, or extend the oral defense. Test the student, never the text.
  • No detector score is ever evidence. Not as a tiebreaker, not "just to check." A number that falsely flags honest non-native writers cannot sit in a disciplinary file.
Real world

Why the last rule is absolute - the record in one paragraph. OpenAI's own detector caught 26% of AI text and was retired for low accuracy. Detectors falsely flagged 61% of honest TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers. Vanderbilt's arithmetic came to ~750 falsely accused students a year at their scale, so they turned the detector off. The full evidence, with citations and the simulator trace, lives in b5.

Self-studyWhen the disclosure itself is the problem3 min read▶

The protocol above handles hidden AI use. Two quieter cases deserve a plan too:

  • Honest but over the tier. A student discloses AI use beyond what the assignment allowed - say, restructuring on a polish-only task. This is the system working: the disclosure line did its job. Grade the rubric row honestly ("disclosed, not within tier"), have the redo conversation, and say thank you for the honesty out loud. Punishing a disclosure harder than a concealment teaches concealment.
  • Disclosed nothing, needed nothing. Students who avoided AI entirely need to hear that the empty disclosure line is fine. The rubric row rewards honesty and in-tier judgment - not AI use itself.

Both cases are why the tier statement, the override line, and the rubric row have to agree with each other word for word. A student should never lose marks to a contradiction between two documents you wrote.
