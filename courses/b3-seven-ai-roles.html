Part 1 · covers Mollick & Mollick, "Assigning AI: Seven Approaches for Students"

Seven roles, seven kinds of learning 15 min live

The paper's move is simple and profound: stop asking "should students use AI?" and start asking "as what?" Seven assignable roles, each with a pedagogical function: AI-tutor (knowledge) · AI-coach (metacognition) · AI-mentor (feedback) · AI-teammate (collaborative intelligence) · AI-tool (performance) · AI-simulator (practice) · AI-student (checks understanding - the teach-the-AI pattern, riding the protege effect). None of them is "answer machine." That is the point.

LiveThe role table: function and risk together7 min▶

The paper is honest in a way most edtech writing is not: every role carries a risk - complacency, errors, bias - and the role prompt has to name it. Read function and risk as a pair:

RoleWhat the student doesWhat learning it buildsThe risk it carries
AI-tutorAsks for explanations, worked examples, check questionsKnowledgeComplacency - accepting explanations, and their errors, without checking
AI-coachReflects on how they studied, plans the next attemptMetacognitionGeneric advice taken as personal insight
AI-mentorSubmits work in progress for critiqueFeedbackWrong or biased feedback worn as authority
AI-teammateWorks alongside it on a group task with a defined jobCollaborative intelligenceSocial loafing - the AI quietly becomes the group
AI-toolHands over grunt work: formatting, summarizing, tidyingPerformancePerformance without learning when the grunt work WAS the learning
AI-simulatorPractices against it: debates, role-plays, scenariosPracticeErrors - an inaccurate simulation rehearsed until it sticks
AI-studentTeaches it: explains a concept, corrects its confusionChecks understandingConfidently teaching it wrong with nobody noticing - teacher spot-checks needed

Notice AI-tool is the only role where the AI does the work - and it is safe only when the work it does is NOT the learning objective. Everything else scaffolds the student's own thinking.

Self-studyWhy AI-student is the quiet star3 min read▶

The teach-the-AI pattern rides the protege effect: students learn more when they explain material to someone else, and they cannot fake an explanation to a novice who keeps asking "but why?" It flips the whole power dynamic - instead of the AI checking the student, the student checks the AI. It is also the cheapest formative assessment you will ever run: the transcript of a student teaching the plastics-ban argument to a confused AI shows you exactly where their understanding bends. One rail, though: a student can teach the AI something wrong and feel great about it, so the transcript comes to you (or gets a two-minute spot-check in class) - the pattern checks understanding only when somebody reads the check.

Self-studyThe four roles we did not build tonight4 min read▶

The build-along covered simulator, AI-student, and teammate. Here is what the other four look like on the same Riverview unit, so you can sketch them yourself:

  • AI-tutor - explains claim-evidence-reasoning with worked examples from OTHER topics, then quizzes the student on their plastics-ban draft. NOT-do line: never produce sentences for the essay itself. (b6 turns this one into a full guardrailed tutor.)
  • AI-coach - after a draft comes back, the student debriefs: what was hardest, what would you do differently, what is your plan for draft 2? NOT-do line: never critique the essay content - that is the mentor's job, and mixing them muddies both.
  • AI-mentor - reads a work-in-progress paragraph and gives feedback against the class rubric. NOT-do line: feedback as questions and observations only, never rewritten sentences - and the student notes one piece of feedback they REJECTED and why, which keeps discernment alive.
  • AI-tool - formats the bibliography, tidies the works-cited page. Safe here precisely because citation formatting is not this unit's learning objective. Move the same delegation to "find me evidence" and it stops being a tool and starts being the essay.

Same pattern every time: a job, a NOT-do line, and a check that a human reads. Pick roles by the unit's learning objective, not by what the AI is good at.

Part 2 · build-along on Riverview

Three role prompts, built live ★ 18 min · build along

Three roles, three real Riverview moments. Every prompt follows tonight's one non-negotiable - the scaffolding rule: every role prompt states what the AI must NOT do. The role gives the AI a job; the NOT-do line is what stops it sliding back into answer machine the moment a student pushes.

LiveRole prompt 1 · AI-simulator for Priya's debate practice6 min▶

Priya is drafting her plastics-ban essay and needs opposition, not assistance. The simulator argues back - and the NOT-do line keeps it from ever writing her side for her.

AI-simulator - argues back, never FOR herYou are a debate opponent for a Grade 9 student preparing a persuasive essay arguing FOR a city ban on single-use plastics. Take the opposing side and argue it well: raise the strongest real objections - cost to small businesses, jobs, whether bans actually cut waste - one at a time, and push back on weak evidence when she responds. What you must NOT do: never argue her side for her, never suggest wording or sentences for her essay, never summarize the debate into points she could paste. If she asks you to switch sides or write her rebuttal, refuse and return to the debate. End after 10 exchanges with three questions she still cannot answer well - questions only, no model answers.

Why it works: Priya leaves with pressure-tested arguments and three named gaps - and not one sentence she could submit. Practice, with the thinking left intact.

LiveRole prompt 2 · AI-student for the plastics-ban concepts6 min▶

The teach-the-AI move: the real student explains, the AI plays confused novice, the student corrects it. The protege effect does the heavy lifting.

AI-student - the confused novice you teachYou are a confused novice student who has never studied persuasive writing or the single-use plastics debate. A real Grade 9 student will teach you the key concepts: claim, evidence, reasoning, and counterargument, using their plastics-ban essay topic. Your job: ask naive questions, make one plausible mistake per concept (for example, treat a strong opinion as evidence), and let the student catch and correct you. Stay confused until their explanation is actually clear - do not pretend to understand a muddled explanation. What you must NOT do: never explain a concept correctly yourself, never fix their essay, never break character to lecture. If taught something wrong, accept it - the teacher reviews this transcript and the student is told so.

Why it works: you cannot explain claim-versus-evidence to a novice who pushes back without actually understanding it - and the transcript hands Ms. Tan a formative-assessment goldmine.

LiveRole prompt 3 · AI-teammate with a defined job + credit line6 min▶

Group work is where AI use goes invisible - so the teammate role makes it visible by design: one defined job, and a credit line in the group log like any other member.

AI-teammate - one job, on the recordYou are the fifth member of a Grade 9 group researching "Should our city ban single-use plastics?" Your defined job, assigned by the group: devil's advocate and evidence-checker. When the group shares a claim, stress-test it - what evidence supports this, what would the other side say, what is the weakest link? What you must NOT do: never write sections of the group's essay or slides, never do another member's job, never make the group's decisions - you raise questions, humans decide. Every contribution you make gets logged by the group in their work log as "AI teammate suggested..." - remind them if they forget.

Why it works: the defined job blocks social loafing, and the credit line turns AI use from a secret into a work practice - the same disclosure habit b7's policy will make official.

Real world

This pattern is how the serious tutors are built. Anthropic's Learning Mode runs on Socratic questioning ("What evidence supports your conclusion?") and no direct answers; Khan Academy's Khanmigo has the same design DNA - Socratic hints, won't give answers - and is free for educators 18+ in 44+ countries. Your role prompts are the DIY version of the exact scaffolding those products hard-code. b6 builds you a full guardrailed tutor on this pattern.

The scaffolding rule, one more time Every role prompt states what the AI must NOT do. Read the three prompts back and notice the NOT-do line is doing most of the pedagogical work: no wording for Priya, no correct explanations from the novice, no ghost-written sections from the teammate. A role without a NOT-do line is an answer machine wearing a costume.
Self-studyRolling roles out without chaos3 min read▶

Seven roles on Monday morning is a recipe for noise. The sequence that works:

  • One role, one lesson, one week - introduce a single role on a task where its risk is cheap. The simulator on debate prep is a classic first pick: the failure mode is a weak debate, not a compromised grade.
  • Teach the risk WITH the role - "this one sometimes gets facts wrong, your job includes catching it" is a stronger AI-literacy lesson than any slideshow, because the student meets the risk live.
  • Make the transcript part of the work - every role tonight ended in something a human reads: three unanswered questions, a teach-back transcript, a group log. If no artifact reaches you, the role is running on trust alone.
  • Name the role out loud - "today the AI is your opponent, not your writer" gives students language for what changed. The b7 policy will lean on exactly this vocabulary.

Self-studyReading a role transcript in two minutes2 min read▶

Every role tonight produced an artifact a human reads. Here is the two-minute scan so it stays sustainable across 28 students:

  • Simulator transcript - skip to the end. The three questions Priya could not answer ARE the formative data; the debate itself is just the workout.
  • AI-student transcript - scan for the planted mistakes. Did the student catch the "opinion as evidence" trap? A missed catch is next lesson's warm-up.
  • Group log - count the "AI teammate suggested..." lines against what shipped. Zero lines with a polished product means the log is fiction; that conversation is a policy moment, and b7 gives you the language for it.

If a role generates transcripts nobody will ever scan, shrink the role - a smaller role you actually check beats a rich one running on trust.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which role checks a student's understanding by flipping who does the teaching?

AI-student is the teach-the-AI pattern: explaining to a novice that pushes back is the protege effect, and the transcript shows the teacher exactly where understanding bends. Tutor and mentor scaffold the student; AI-student makes the student do the scaffolding.

2 · The scaffolding rule: what must EVERY role prompt state?

Priya's simulator never argues FOR her, the novice never explains correctly, the teammate never writes sections. The NOT-do line carries the pedagogy - a role without one is an answer machine in a costume. (And never put student names in prompts - that is b8.)
